## Twenty times the same video

In 2018, two researchers at the University of Chicago had about two hundred people watch a video of someone throwing darts. Some saw it once and others twenty times. Those who watched it twenty times expected to score more points, 43.6 against 34.2 on average, but when they threw they scored less: 21.2 against 26.8 (Kardas and O'Brien, 2018).

That's why I stopped watching System Design videos. System Design is the design of large software systems and a classic topic in job interviews. I was learning passively and it was a big waste of time. I may have known it in theory, but when it came down to it, I didn't. When I want to learn something hard, the phrase that guides me is learn by doing: try first, see what fails, ask for feedback and try again.

## Knowing it in theory

The feeling of learning misleads in both directions. In 2019, a team at Harvard taught two physics topics to 149 students, one as a lecture and the other as an active class, where students solved problems in groups and the instructor corrected them. With the active class they learned almost half a standard deviation more, and at the same time said they felt they had learned less (Deslauriers et al., 2019). Listening to someone who explains well feels like learning, and making mistakes feels like not getting anywhere, even when the results say otherwise.

The same shows up when many courses are put together. In 2014, Scott Freeman and his team at the University of Washington pooled 225 studies of university courses in science, engineering and math. With active learning, the average failure rate was 21.8%; with lectures, 33.8%. Students who only listened were 1.5 times more likely to fail, and exam scores rose by about 6% with active learning (Freeman et al., 2014).

What has let me understand things deeply is the experience of having done them, of having failed and of trying again until I got them right. I've tried many things and failed a great deal, and that has taught me why some ways are better than others and in what context. You never learn something completely, there's always something better to do, and every attempt adds to that experience.

What follows is how I do it, with a finding for each part and what you could try in your own context.

## 1. Try before you study

A study session of mine starts by figuring out the first step I can take. With a surface-level overview I start doing things. As soon as I get bored of reading, I move to action: I try to build an app, say my first words in the language, write a text in it or, if I'm learning chess, try the new opening in a live game. The idea is to learn something and use it right away. When I fail and it seems the first attempt was useless, I study a little to improve the next one, and that's how I keep building on that first call to action.

The first attempt leaves me with my own intuition of what it feels like, how it works, what the details are and what I can improve. With that intuition, the reading or the video that comes next serves me much better, because it guides me from the point where I started.

This has been measured too. In 2021, Tanmay Sinha and Manu Kapur, at ETH Zurich, pooled 53 studies with 166 comparisons between two orders: explanation first and problems after, or problems first and explanation after. Trying first had a moderate effect in its favor for understanding concepts and applying them to new cases, larger when the design let students make mistakes on purpose before the explanation (Sinha and Kapur, 2021). For learning a step-by-step procedure there was no difference, and with primary school children explaining first worked slightly better.

If you want to learn something, try doing it before watching the whole tutorial, even if it goes badly, and use the tutorial afterward to correct what you already tried.

## 2. A little harder than you can do

If what we try is too hard, we get frustrated and quit. If it's slightly beyond our ability, that's when we start to improve. Programming, swimming or a language are learned in small steps that feel a bit hard, but not so hard that we get frustrated.

In 2019, a team from the University of Arizona, Brown and Princeton calculated how often it pays to fail in order to learn fastest, in mathematical models of learning and in simulations. The optimum was failing about 15% of the time, that is, getting 85% right (Wilson et al., 2019). The authors leave open whether the number holds for people; the idea of a fair difficulty, adjusted as you improve, matches what I've lived.

To someone in my family who wanted to learn something hard I'd say to take a deep, immersive dive, at their own measure. For a language, switch the phone to the language you want to learn and read in it. At first it's tough and hard, and that's why the difficulty has to match your level.

## 3. Changing a pattern with repetition

In competitive programming I made the same mistake three times, in three different problems, and that's when I started doing spaced repetition. When I fail at something I do often, it's usually a pattern I have ingrained, like filler words when speaking. You get rid of them by replacing them with other patterns and by noticing the moments when you use them.

Two findings explain why repetition works. The first is that recalling without help leaves more than rereading. In 2006, at Washington University in St. Louis, people who read a text four times remembered 40% a week later, and those who read it once and tried to recall it three times, 61%. The ones who reread believed they would remember more (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006). The second is that it pays to let time pass between reviews. In a study with 1,354 people, to remember something a week later the best gap between reviews was about three days, and to remember it a year later, about four weeks (Cepeda et al., 2008).

A third finding may help explain mistakes like mine. In 2007, Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor, at the University of South Florida, had college students practice geometry problems in two ways: one type of problem in a row, in a block, or several types mixed. During practice, the block group got 89% right and the mixers 60%. A week later, on the test, it flipped: 63% for the mixers and 20% for the block group (Rohrer and Taylor, 2007). It was only 18 students, so it's a clue more than a law, but it makes sense, because practicing one pattern in a row helps you solve it and leaves untrained the moment of recognizing when to use it.

If you notice you keep making the same mistake, it's worth writing it down and coming back to that kind of problem a few days later, without looking at the solution, until the new pattern comes out on its own.

## 4. Feedback that puts your assumptions on the table

Claude gives me the first overview, some parameters to start practicing and corrects me once I've made the first attempt. The most valuable part is that it can correct with an analysis, sometimes a statistical one: it builds simulations or uses code to check the ideas I brought with me, what statisticians call *priors*. That makes the feedback more accurate and corrects my biases. When something doesn't work, I brainstorm, with Claude or on my own, about which paths I have from where I am and which would be the most useful.

That's what happened with triathlon. Analyzing my data with Claude, I understood that the biggest weakness of my first year had been nutrition. My first thought had been to lose weight because that would make me faster, and that idea drained the energy I needed to perform, both in training and in races. I brought it from the gym, where you go to lose weight and sometimes train fasted, and that's how a habit from one place spreads to another. In endurance sports the rules change: the goal is to perform well, and in fact my focus was never losing weight, but my assumptions had blinded me to the new context. What mattered was eating well during exercise and outside of it.

That's why I care about saying assumptions out loud. If we don't say them, we carry them; once we identify them, we can decide with data whether they hold or need to change. Try writing down what you believe about what you're learning before asking for feedback, from a coach, from someone who knows or from an AI, and ask them which of those ideas doesn't hold up.

## 5. The first twenty hours

We'll never learn something completely, but we can get fairly good soon. Josh Kaufman, author of *The Personal MBA*, gave a TEDx talk in 2013 that has more than 41 million views. In it he proposes that about twenty hours of focused practice are enough to go from knowing nothing to being reasonably good at almost any skill (Kaufman, 2013). He tells it from his own experience, not from a study, and his method resembles the above: break the skill into parts, learn enough to correct yourself, remove what gets in the way and practice.

I use it as a guide: if I learn the most fundamental patterns, the ones that repeat the most, I can use them most of the time. I'm not an expert, but in those first hours I make enormous progress, which is wonderful.

## After 30

To someone 30 or older who feels they can no longer learn, I'd say the opposite. A baby doesn't know how to learn a language; we already learned one and know how it's done. What happens is that we don't get involved the way a baby does, learning out of necessity and with years to do it. We want results soon and without being immersed in something that demands it.

The data add a nuance. In an MIT study with almost 670,000 people, the ease of learning a language's grammar stayed high until age 17 or 18 and then dropped. To reach the grammar of a native speaker it paid to start before 10 (Hartshorne et al., 2018). The authors themselves say an adult can learn a language very well, though they'll rarely sound native, and that in lab studies adults learn better than children.

What we have to do, then, is create those needs. The brain is a very efficient machine: it already has its paths well defined and tends to reuse them. That's why it helps to do something slightly outside our comfort zone and try new paths that come from a brainstorm, from good guidance or from a coach's feedback. Then we keep practicing, with analysis and reflection on our own context: our assumptions, how we feel and where we want to go.

## So

So what I do is try first. I see what fails, ask for feedback, study just enough for the next attempt and repeat, with steps that feel a little hard. Videos and readings are still there, but they come later, once I have an intuition of my own to build on.

If you want to learn something hard, you don't need to prepare first or watch every video. Start with the first step you can take today, even if it goes badly, and learn by doing.

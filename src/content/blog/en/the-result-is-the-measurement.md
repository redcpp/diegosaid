## The next kilometer

When I was training for the Riviera Nayarit half Ironman, I chased the next meter, the next kilometer, a faster pace. A new view, a new experience, a new challenge. There was pain, fatigue and discipline, but personal improvement paid me back: the dopamine, the endorphins, the connection with other athletes.

I trained all three sports every day, plus gym, physical therapy, sauna and yoga, three times a week each. With bike maintenance, it came to five to eight hours a day. I was going for extraordinary times and finished in 6 hours 20 minutes, the slowest of my group of friends. The volume left me overtrained: I cramped from the swim on, the bike was worse, and I nearly walked the run.

They expected much more from me. So did I. And it felt like a letdown, because I keep aiming for the stars and only reaching the moon.

Today I'm tired of doing things focused on results. I do race for a time, but that isn't the goal. The goal is to get better day by day, have an amazing time and build systems I can repeat without dropping them. The biggest risk I see is losing discipline under the weight of not getting there. I want to be like the Stoics.

## What depends on me

I read Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations*, and two ideas have stayed with me. The first: there are external factors we can't change; the only thing we can change is how we react to them. He puts it this way: when something external pains you, what disturbs you is your own judgment about it, and it is in your power to wipe out that judgment now ([book 8, 47](https://lexundria.com/m_aur_med/8.47/lg)).

The second is the one about the bees. Marcus Aurelius talks to himself on the mornings when it's hard to get up: the plants, the birds, the ants, the spiders and the bees each do their part. And he isn't going to do the work of a human being? ([book 5, 1](https://lexundria.com/m_aur_med/5.1/lg)). In the same way, we have human work to do, and it matters that we do it, because it's our nature.

In a race, the final result doesn't depend 100% on me, because there are other competitors. Each of them is focused on the race and on their own growth.

## The result is the measurement

When your heart is at 200 beats per minute, you're not thinking about the result. You're thinking about the second of pain you're in. You don't care about the news, or celebrity gossip, or whether the new movie is out. All you want is for the suffering to stop.

The finish is a line painted on the ground, infinitesimally thin. We don't race to reach that line.

When I race, the goal is to do better than myself a month ago, a year ago, or when I started. I race to hold on one second longer in that uncomfortable moment when I want to pull out, when I want to give up. I race to go faster on the same effort: for my effort to show up as more strength, more power, a better measurable result. But the result isn't the goal; it's only the measurement.

And I need that measurement. It's very hard for me to stay motivated without a number or some feedback telling me whether I'm getting better or worse. I've abandoned a lot of sports because of that, tennis among them: it can be a lot of fun, and in the long run I might see improvement, but not from one week to the next. I really like seeing continuous progress, and it can even be addictive.

In fact, I often analyze my data after a race that felt terrible and I'm surprised to see I'm actually trending up. Maybe I raced smarter, maybe the race was harder or that week's field was stronger. I can finish worse in the ranking and better in my numbers. That's why I keep the two apart: the ranking also depends on everyone else; my numbers depend on me. You don't see that unless you stop to analyze all the numbers and look at the whole, instead of closing in on a single thing like the result.

Sports research points the same way. A [meta-analysis of 27 studies](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2022.2116723) compared three kinds of goals: process (how you execute: technique, pacing), performance (your own mark) and outcome (your place against others). Process goals had the largest effect on performance, with an effect size of 1.36; outcome goals, a mere 0.09. For reference, 0.8 is already considered a large effect (Williamson and colleagues, 2022).

## I cramped in training

On October 3, in a 4-hour-45-minute race simulation, I cramped at 3 hours 10 minutes, just when I was feeling great. I'm prone to cramps. If it happens in a race, so be it, and I hold firm through it. But it did feel bad to have it happen in training, because I train precisely so it won't, and the session was cut short by an hour and a half.

These sessions are supposed to teach my body to stay firm, steady and fresh in the hard moments. I learned that, however strong the will, sometimes time and conditions don't cooperate. My body isn't yet ready for an effort like the one I demanded of it. I chose to stop rather than hurt it, because on race day I want to be in top shape, and today it isn't worth wrecking it.

Will really shows on race day. Training is a time to grow, a time to learn, not to destroy yourself.

## Enjoying it so I don't depend on discipline

What I enjoy most are Zwift races, an indoor cycling platform. I made them non-negotiable, because this isn't about training only for Cozumel; it's about training for life. I enjoy long rides, but every now and then I also like to push myself to the limit: the adrenaline, feeling that in a sprint I have a shot at the podium.

On Sundays, the race-simulation days, we do look for rigor, and the reason is practical: rigor makes it more scientific. We depend on fewer variables and we're more precise in building what we'll need on race day.

If I'm not enjoying what I do, it's very easy to start looking for excuses. If I'm having fun, I don't need to be disciplined, because I identify with what I do. I wake up and, without hunting for motivation or struggling, I get up glad to have the chance to do what I enjoy most.

When a session feels like an exam, it's mentally very hard. I can be consistent and suffer every day, but there comes a point, a dark place, where our demons want us to quit. If we're not in a place of joy and strength, we're more likely to fail or to run. That's why fun matters: to go through the hard moments by choice, and to build a habit more easily.

The data back this up. In [five studies](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/implement-immediate-rewards-as-you-pursue-that-long-term-goal) on New Year's resolutions, exercise and studying, people said they pursued those goals for the future payoff, like health. Even so, what best predicted who kept going was the immediate reward: enjoying the activity (Woolley and Fishbach, 2017).

The habit I've kept up these nine weeks almost without thinking is the weekly race. I get on the bike with my ritual: my breakfast, my coffee. That routine puts me in the right frame of mind to get into flow as soon as the race starts.

Building a habit like that takes time. In a [University College London study](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674), a new habit took a median of 66 days to become automatic, ranging from 18 to 254 depending on the person. Missing a day didn't meaningfully change the process (Lally and colleagues, 2010).

To someone who doesn't train, I'd say they have to identify with what they do, and to get there they first have to enjoy it. If you offer a cigarette to someone who doesn't smoke, it's very easy for them to be "disciplined" and say no, because their identity is tied to being a person who doesn't smoke. The example is James Clear's, from *Atomic Habits*. A [2012 study](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-663212.html), with small groups, found something similar: of those who told themselves "I don't," 8 in 10 stuck with their goal for all 10 days; of those who told themselves "I can't," only 1 in 10.

The same goes for what I leave out. I don't have Instagram, Facebook or TikTok. I blocked Reddit and YouTube Shorts, and deleted the apps that waste my time from my phone. I even deleted the chess app: I enjoy a game now and then, but it tires the mind, and becoming a great chess player isn't part of my plan. The 30 minutes I could give to chess I'd rather give to competitive programming, which defines me much more.

## Six fronts

Today is different. I still train, but my athletic focus is cycling. I'm a better cyclist than when I did half Ironmans and ultramarathons, though a worse runner and swimmer. Cycling has a huge amount of statistics and a lot of strategy, and both fill me with joy off the bike, because I can analyze my files and improve for the next race.

I now have a family and more responsibilities, but the foundation is still there. The person I became training for the Ironman puts all his energy and discipline into several things at once. In the Ironman it was three sports plus physical therapy, gym and nutrition: six fronts I had to coordinate.

Outside sport I apply the same discipline and the same mindset of fun. In competitive programming, no matter what, every Saturday I do a small hour-and-a-half contest; if I can't, I still try to solve a problem here and there during the week. In writing, I realized that by dictating my thoughts I enjoy the process more, because I don't lose the thread typing out what I want to say. And running a company, I try to build continuous feedback systems, like meaningful conversations per week or the conversion rate.

## Two laps of the island

On November 8 I ride [GFNY Cozumel](https://www.granfondoguide.com/Contents/Index/9245/registration-open-for-2026-gfny-cozumel-the-original-gfny-world-classic), a cycling gran fondo around the island. What I want is to finish feeling good: no cramps, good watts, heart rate under control and without suffering much. To enjoy the views and the company, and to celebrate the process I've kept up for so long.

It's not a day for racing all out. I'm not going for first place or the podium; I'm far from that, especially in races this long. We aim for the stars, but if we only reach the moon, that's fine: we should say so, and also count how far we've come. We're just going to have a great time and be an inspiration for my family and friends, who are coming with me: they'll ride one lap of the island and I'll ride two.

A few months ago, two laps of Cozumel, 154 kilometers, seemed crazy to me, because for me that's five hours. Now I see it as possible if I'm smart with my efforts. Sometimes I need to raise the bar to gain confidence and feel I can take on any challenge I set myself.

This training cycle is about transformation: overcoming different obstacles bit by bit until I become someone better than I was. That will seep into many areas of my life, because it makes me feel better and gives me mental clarity. When I face problems somewhere else, I know they're a step toward bigger achievements.

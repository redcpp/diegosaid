## The temptation of idle tokens

At first I asked Claude to write for me at night, with idle tokens. A token is the unit of text a language model works with; idle tokens are the capacity I have available that nobody uses while I sleep.

It's something to keep very much in mind when creating with artificial intelligence: unlike us, AI doesn't sleep. We have a very capable technology, and not having it work 24/7 feels like a waste.

But at the same time it's a reactive technology. It doesn't take the initiative, at least not for now, and it needs us to give it a direction. With the right direction we can go down virtually any path. It still has its limitations and some rough edges, but in practice, yes.

That was my initial motivation. What made me the one dictating now is exactly that need to give it a direction, and above all the need to share more as a person. In the age of artificial intelligence, what we look for, and will look for more and more, is interaction with other humans. Humanity is an increasingly valuable resource, and we have to be more human.

## What loses value: text

Today creating text costs very little, and there is text everywhere. One example: in 2025, 3,529,980 self-published titles were registered in the United States, 38.7% more than the year before, according to [Bowker](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20260323/99943-book-output-topped-4-million-in-2025.html). That same year, print book sales grew just 0.3%, to 762.4 million units, according to [Circana BookScan](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industry-news/bookselling/article/99417-print-book-sales-rose-slightly-in-2025.html). Not all of that growth is AI, because Bowker counts registrations and the same book in several formats counts several times, but the gap between what gets published and what gets read is real.

There is less and less content written by hand, and that's a problem for anyone looking for connection, for anyone looking for firsthand experience. Content is more and more synthetic, and AIs are trained on that content too.

That has a cost for the AIs themselves. A study published in [*Nature* in 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y) showed that training models, generation after generation, on text produced by other models degrades them: they gradually forget what's uncommon. In one of its tests, a text about medieval architecture ended up, by the ninth generation, as a list of jackrabbits. The authors don't say training on synthetic data is impossible, but they do say it has to be filtered very carefully, and that data from genuine human interactions will be increasingly valuable.

That's why the companies behind AI value original human-written work more: they need it for training. And there isn't that much of it. [Epoch AI estimates (2024)](https://epoch.ai/blog/will-we-run-out-of-data) that all quality public human text adds up to about 300 trillion tokens, and that models will have used it up at some point between 2026 and 2032.

## What gains value: humanity

Artificial intelligences leave their mark when they write. They leave their essence, a synthetic essence. And essence is exactly what we share when we write, when we speak, when we think. We are unique beings, and we have to respect that for our own sake.

For a while we came to value the inhuman: abstractions. Language is human, yes, but this isn't only about writing. It's about sharing human ideas, grounded in the senses and in experience. That's what all of this is about.

I no longer even need to look for AI-made text in magazines or online newspapers. I find it in conversations with friends and in the school group chat, places where you'd expect something more than a meat proxy on the other side: someone who just forwards whatever a machine answered them. It's an everyday experience. More and more we take refuge in the synthetic essence and stop sharing our human essence. And people resent it: in a [YouGov and Meltwater survey (2026)](https://www.meltwater.com/en/press-releases/consumer-perception-of-generative-ai), 32% said they would trust a brand less if its content were AI-generated, against 15% who would trust it more.

These days I really enjoy YouTube content made by people, like 3Blue1Brown or Numberphile. I enjoy even more reading on a blog, like Financial Samurai, the direct experiences of someone who went through a hero's journey: someone who fought through hardship and came out victorious in the end. They give us their knowledge and their experience, and they light up new paths for us to take.

A summary gives you the general idea, but it doesn't help you feel the emotions of going through adversity. Sébastien Bubeck, a researcher at OpenAI, put it this way at a [symposium on the future of mathematics at Stanford in May 2026](https://stanforddaily.com/2026/05/06/future-of-mathematics-symposium-2026/): maybe in a few years we can outsource thinking, but understanding can't be outsourced; everyone has to understand for themselves.

## The irony: an AI helps me write this

I dictated this text as I was thinking it. It's ironic that an artificial intelligence helps me write it, so it's worth saying what it helps me with. It's two things.

The first is transcribing what I say and turning it into text. The voice is a continuum, and text loses a lot when it makes it discrete: it loses intonation, emphasis, emotion. The best way to share my ideas would be spoken, but that would make it a podcast, and I'm not sure that's what I'm after. I want something a computer can understand too, something that works as a record for my own future use, or that reaches others through something like a post.

The second is organizing and structuring my ideas in a way that's less chaotic and more deterministic. It also works as a “second pair of eyes” that lets me see what my own biased view doesn't let me notice.

Saying so matters: in the same YouGov and Meltwater survey, 86% of people said AI-generated content should be disclosed.

## What I'm living, and the failures

That hero's journey is exactly where I am today. I'm fighting through adversity, finding my place, organizing my time and my experiences, sharing them, being part of the world around me and taking care of my family. All of that is lived firsthand, and no artificial intelligence could write it for me.

What I most want to tell are the lessons. What's hardest to tell are the failures, because there are some, and many; several I'm still embarrassed to talk about. But without them you can't learn.

One of them: I've been studying languages for years and haven't managed to learn them as well as I'd like. With Russian, no matter how hard I try, I don't get past the basics. I bought children's books and tried Duolingo, but I struggle a lot to understand and remember the vocabulary.

German is a language I like a lot, but when I got to the grammar I hit a wall. It's complex and takes dedication, time and effort. That's where I gave up: instead of doubling down or studying smarter, I decided to set it aside to keep going with other projects.

Terence Tao, one of the most important mathematicians of our time, points to something similar about mathematical proofs written by an AI: because they're [too polished](https://the-decoder.com/terence-tao-says-ai-could-trigger-maths-biggest-crisis-since-godel/), they're easy to read and hard to learn from, while the mistakes in a human explanation can genuinely help whoever reads it.

## If nobody reads me a year from now

Attention is the central asset of an era in which creating text costs very little. Everything in the information age is fighting for your attention.

And yet I'm not doing this for attention. I'm not after the largest number of subscribers, although that would be motivating. If nobody reads me a year from now, at least I created.

We spend most of the day absorbing and rarely create. We watch TV, we listen to the radio, and surely we all have a lot to say; sometimes there's someone who would like to hear it. Even if nobody reads what I want to say, it's worth using my skills, putting my ideas in order and remembering what I lived. Reflecting. Reflection is both the end and the means.

It's like racing a triathlon. Yes, we go for times, but we don't race to reach a line marked on the ground. The course is the goal: we race to live the experience of being surrounded by people with the same interests and the same motivation, to get better, to feel good, to share the day. That's what I care about.

Why do I write, if ChatGPT already knows everything? Very simple. I write for myself, for the people close to me, to connect, and to share the things ChatGPT cannot experience. To share a human experience.
